AI vs AI: How Cyber Defence is Being Rewritten

Talking points / brief explanation + key background information
AI is fundamentally changing both sides of cybersecurity. Advanced AI models are becoming increasingly capable of performing complex digital tasks autonomously. These capabilities can be used to identify vulnerabilities, analyse systems, develop attack paths and support cyber operations. At the same time, AI offers significant opportunities to strengthen cyber defence through automated monitoring, anomaly detection, vulnerability management and incident response.
We are moving towards an “AI vs AI” cybersecurity environment. Attackers can use AI to operate faster, at greater scale and with increasing levels of autonomy. Our cyber defences will therefore also need to become faster, smarter and increasingly AI-enabled. The key challenge is clear: we cannot effectively counter AI-driven attacks operating at machine speed with defences that rely solely on human speed and intervention.
This requires us to rethink how we protect government information systems. Traditional cybersecurity remains essential, but security architectures will increasingly need to anticipate AI-enabled and potentially autonomous attacks. Governments should assess whether existing security controls, SOC capabilities and incident-response processes remain effective when adversaries can use AI to continuously identify and exploit weaknesses.
AI should not only be considered a threat; it can also become an important defensive capability. AI can help security teams analyse large volumes of security data, detect patterns that humans may overlook, prioritize vulnerabilities and respond more rapidly to incidents. Governments should therefore actively explore how AI can responsibly strengthen their cyber defence capabilities.
AI itself also creates a new attack surface. Models, AI agents, training and operational data, interfaces and supporting infrastructure must be protected. Security-by-design principles should therefore apply to AI systems throughout their lifecycle. This includes testing, monitoring, access control, logging and protection against manipulation of models and agents.
The role of human control needs to be reconsidered. Human oversight and accountability remain essential, particularly in government. However, in an environment where attacks may unfold within seconds, requiring human approval for every defensive action may become impractical. We therefore need to determine which defensive actions AI may perform autonomously, under which predefined conditions, and when human authorization must remain mandatory.
Governments need sufficient knowledge and independent testing capabilities. As governments become increasingly dependent on advanced AI models and AI-enabled security solutions, we should be able to independently assess their security, reliability and resilience. Governments should consider whether additional national or shared international  capabilities are required to test advanced AI models and AI-security applications.
International cooperation will become even more important. AI-enabled cyber threats do not respect national borders. Governments should strengthen cooperation on threat intelligence, testing methodologies, standards, red-teaming and potentially shared AI-enabled defensive capabilities. International cooperation can help prevent individual governments from having to develop all of these capabilities independently.
Key message for the CIO Rijk
AI is not only changing our information systems; it is changing the way those systems are attacked and defended.
Governments therefore need to approach AI from two perspectives: AI as a threat and AI as a defence capability. We need to protect our digital infrastructure against adversaries using AI, while at the same time learning how to use AI responsibly to strengthen our own cyber defence.
The challenge is not simply to choose between human control and autonomous AI. The challenge is to design security architectures in which AI can act autonomously within clearly defined boundaries, while humans remain responsible for setting those boundaries, managing exceptions and ensuring accountability.
Ultimately, the strategic question is:
How do we remain in control of cyber defence when both the attacker and the defender increasingly operate at machine speed?
Five questions that could be raised during the session
Are our current cybersecurity frameworks and security architectures sufficiently prepared for attackers operating at machine speed and with increasing levels of autonomy?
How much autonomy should we give AI systems to defend government networks? For example, should AI be allowed to autonomously block accounts, isolate network segments or take systems offline when an attack is detected?
Where should the human remain “in the loop”, and where could mandatory human intervention make our response too slow to counter an AI-enabled attack effectively?
Do governments need their own independent capabilities to test and evaluate advanced AI models and AI-enabled security solutions, or can we rely sufficiently on technology providers and existing certification mechanisms?
How can governments cooperate internationally on AI-enabled cyber defence? Could we move beyond sharing cyber threat intelligence towards sharing testing capabilities, defensive AI models, methodologies and AI-enabled cyber defence capabilities?
